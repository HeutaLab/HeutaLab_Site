PlanBench.

Plan once, teach more. A primary technology planner built around the planning format one school actually uses — by the teacher who had to fill it in.

Every school has a planning format. It arrives as a Word document with nine headings and a table for the weeks, and it is filled in the same way every term: open last term's copy, rename it, overwrite the bits that changed, lose the link to the resources somewhere around week four. PlanBench is that document turned into an app. The headings are the same. What changes is that the resources stay clipped to the week they belong to, the assessment criteria go somewhere useful, and the finished plan comes out the other end as the Word or PDF the school wanted in the first place.

It is also the fourth app on this site for a reason beyond planning. It is the clearest demonstration we have that a teacher can now build the tool their own job needs, rather than wait for a vendor to guess at it. More on that below.

Open PlanBench Why a teacher built it

Nine sections, in the order you would think them through

A plan is nine sections, and the sidebar shows which are done. Each one can be typed straight in, or drafted by AI from the title and whatever you have already written, then corrected. The sections are the school's, not ours; they are the ones a good unit plan needs whether or not anyone asks for them.

SectionWhat goes in it
1 · Project overviewTitle, year group, term, technology focus, the final outcome, lesson length and frequency, and why the unit matters beyond the classroom.
2 · Foundational knowledge & misconceptionsWhat the children need to know first, and the pitfalls you already know they will fall into.
3 · Core challenge & success criteriaThe learning intentions and what secure work looks like.
4 · Scaffolding & support resourcesEvery scaffold, with its link, and a column to record whether it was actually used.
5 · Practice designGuided, independent and fluent practice for each strand, plus spaced, retrieval and deliberate practice.
6 · Lesson spiralOne job ticket per week: prior knowledge activated, new learning, activities, evidence, notes, and the resources clipped on.
7 · AssessmentCriteria in your wording and the child's, in-lesson checks pinned to a week, and the end-of-unit task and end point.
8 · Metacognition & transferHow the children will think about their own learning, and where it goes next.
9 · Monitoring & reflectionWhat you will look at to know whether it worked, and what to change next year.

The week is the unit of work

The lesson spiral is where PlanBench earns its keep. Each week is a job ticket with the same five fields, and each ticket is stamped with where it sits in the unit — guided at the start, partial fade through the middle, independent, then reflect, share and showcase at the end. Add a ninth week and the stamps move. Change the project length on the desk and the whole spiral re-cuts itself.

  • Resources clip to the week. A link, a video or a screenshot is attached to the ticket it is used in, so the week-four exemplar is on the week-four ticket and not in a folder called Resources (new) FINAL.
  • A pupil page for every week — the child-facing version of the ticket, and a differentiate button that drafts the support and stretch for that week.
  • Gates, not grades. A gate is a checklist a child must pass before moving on, and a return costs something specific. The plan records what.

Assessment that goes somewhere

Most planning formats have an assessment box that is filled in once and never read again. PlanBench makes the criteria do work. Each one is written twice — as you would judge it, and as the child would read it — and the child's column is what becomes the class checklist.

  • Share the checklist with the class as a link, or download it as a Word document.
  • Download the criteria as a rubric sheet, ready for moderation.
  • Send the criteria straight to Creance, so the marking grid for the unit already exists before the first lesson.
  • Pin quick in-lesson checks to a week — what you will ask, and what a secure answer shows — and set the end point for the whole unit as working towards, expected and greater depth.

Out of the door as the document the school asked for

Nobody else has to use PlanBench for you to use it. A finished plan previews as a clean document with the nine sections as tables, and leaves as a Word file, a PDF, or a Word file laid out on the school's own template. It also goes the other way: an existing plan in Word can be imported and carried on in the app.

  • Year overview pins every plan on the desk to a grid of terms by year group, so the gaps in coverage are visible before September.
  • Share links hand a read-only copy of one plan to a colleague. They expire after a year and can be revoked sooner.
  • Connect a folder saves each plan as a file in a real folder — inside OneDrive or Google Drive, say — so the plans follow you between machines without an account. Chrome and Edge only; other browsers keep the plans in the browser instead.

Drafting with AI, inside your own policies

Every section has a Draft this section button, and the desk has a job sheet: type Year 4 · stop-motion animation with iPads and it drafts the whole plan for you to argue with. What makes this different from pasting the same request into a chatbot is the context shelf. Upload the behaviour policy, the curriculum map, the scheme of work, the school's own guidance on marking, and every draft is written within them rather than from a generic idea of what a primary school is.

How the AI is paid for: PlanBench does not run a server that talks to an AI on your behalf. You paste your own Anthropic API key into the desk, it is stored on that device only, and each draft is billed to you at API rates, and the bill is usually small. No key, no drafting; everything else in the app still works. When you press draft, the plan text and the shelf documents you have switched on are sent to Anthropic with that key, and nowhere else.

The point of the fourth app

You could build this too

PlanBench was not designed as a product. It started as one teacher's planning format — the nine headings above, in a Word document, at a real school — and the wish that filling it in each term took less of the week. It was built in the gaps of a timetable by describing, in plain English, what the document needed to do, and correcting the result until it did it. No computer science degree was involved.

That is the thing worth taking from this page, whether or not you ever open the app. The tools that let a teacher describe a piece of software and have it built are now good enough to make the software that your job needs: the tracker for the thing your school tracks, the checklist your department uses, the planner that matches your format rather than a vendor's. What you need is not the ability to code. It is the ability to say precisely what the job is, which is a thing teachers are already good at.

If you want to try, start where PlanBench started: with the document you already fill in, and a sentence about what annoys you about it. The story of how the classroom screen got built is on the blog, and the about page is where to write if you would like a hand.

What is on the device, and what is not

Plans, screenshots, desk settings and the shelf documents live in the browser on the device they were typed into, or in the folder you connected. There are no accounts and nothing is uploaded in the ordinary course of planning. Two things do leave the device, both on purpose and both by you: a share link puts a read-only copy of that one plan on the HeutaLab server until it expires or you revoke it, and an AI draft sends the plan and your switched-on shelf documents to Anthropic with your own key.

Worth knowing before you rely on it: a browser that blocks site storage, or a machine that is wiped nightly, will take the plans with it. The Back up everything button writes one file holding every plan, image, setting and shelf document — use it, and connect a folder if your browser allows it.

Try it

The desk has a button that adds a complete sample unit — Year 5 digital citizenship, eight weeks, all nine sections filled — so you can see a finished plan before writing one. Preview it, export it as Word, and see whether it looks like something your school would accept.
