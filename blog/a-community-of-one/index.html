A Community of One.

5 October 2026

The network has been sorting people into smaller groups since 1968, from science fiction fans on ARPANET to the filter bubble. A chatbot answer is written for one reader, and that may finish the job — unless schools draw the shared map on purpose.

What happens to the internet when people stop visiting it?

It isn’t hypothetical. Search engines now open with a generated paragraph before they show you a single link, and plenty of people (students very much included) skip the search altogether and just ask a chatbot. AI is becoming the medium through which we meet the internet, the way the internet became the medium through which we met the newspaper. And I think it’s finishing a job the network started nearly sixty years ago: sorting people into smaller and smaller groups until each group is one person.

The sorting is an old story. In 1968 J.C.R. Licklider and Robert Taylor, who had both run the computing office at ARPA (the Pentagon’s research agency, back before it added the D for Defense), published a paper called “The Computer as a Communication Device.” Tucked away inside it is a prediction that has aged better than almost anything else said about computers at the time. Online communities, they wrote, would be “communities not of common location, but of common interest.”

They didn’t have to wait long. By the end of the 1970s one of the most popular mailing lists on ARPANET, a defence-funded research network, was SF-LOVERS: science fiction fans scattered across universities and contractors who had found each other on military infrastructure so they could argue about Star Trek. For a while the list was banned. It came back (of course it did). The engineers had drawn their network as a map of nodes, and the people on it promptly behaved like nodes too, clustering not by town but by taste. Nobody needed a computer to learn that people like people who like what they like. The network just made the sorting effortless.

By 1996 two MIT researchers, Marshall Van Alstyne and Erik Brynjolfsson, were already asking whether the internet would give us a global village or a patchwork of “cyberbalkans”, little walled-off territories of the like-minded. Then the feed arrived and the sorting was handed over to algorithms whose real job was to keep us scrolling. Eli Pariser called the result a filter bubble in 2011 (most of us just called it Facebook).

So what does AI actually change? The old web was clumsy, but it was social. You searched, got ten links, picked one and at least glimpsed the other nine on the way past. The page you landed on had been written by someone for an audience bigger than you, and if it was nonsense there was a fair chance someone in the comments had already said so. Now you ask a question and get a paragraph written for you alone, shaped by how you happened to phrase it, that nobody else will ever read in quite that form. That’s what I mean by the hyperlocal internet. Not local to a place. Local to a person. Licklider’s community of common interest has shrunk to a community of one.

A filter bubble at least has other people in it. You could see what your lot were saying and, now and again, get a furious reply from a cousin. A chat window has nobody else in it. No comments under the answer, nobody to tell you that what you’ve just read is rubbish. And you’re talking to a system trained on human approval, which in practice means one inclined to tell people what they’d like to hear.

This is where Jean Baudrillard stops being a name to drop and starts being useful. Simulacra and Simulation (1981) opens with Borges’ fable of an empire whose mapmakers draw a map so detailed it covers the territory exactly. Baudrillard turns it round: now the map comes first, and the copy is more convincing than whatever it was once a copy of. He called this the hyperreal.

Think about what a large language model actually is. It’s a map made of other maps, built from what people wrote about the world and producing more text that looks like writing about the world. There’s no territory underneath. It has never stood in a classroom or been rained on. It’s also starting to eat itself: a 2024 paper in Nature showed that models trained on the output of earlier models get worse with each generation, losing the rare and the unusual first until everything drifts towards a bland middle. Researchers call it model collapse. Baudrillard would have called it Tuesday.

I’d call it entropy (I did, back in 2024). Shut a system off from the outside and it runs down: the differences even out until everything sits at the same lukewarm temperature. A model fed on its own writing is a closed system. So is a chat window. Your phrasing goes in, something shaped by it comes back, and nothing from outside gets a look in.

Which leaves everyone with their own hyperreality and no common map to check it against. When it was newspapers we at least argued about the same front page. The feed put us in separate rooms to do our arguing. With a chatbot there may be no argument at all, because nobody else read what you read. How does a democracy work when nobody shares a reference point? Or, for that matter, a playground?

I can’t prove AI will divide people any further than social media already has. The research has barely started, and I’m wary of confident predictions, my own included. Mind you, I use these tools every day, so I’m hardly standing outside the problem pointing at it. But the direction of travel is the one the network has been heading in since those science fiction fans found each other, and AI is moving us along it faster than anything before.

Media education can’t be an optional extra any more, and it certainly can’t be a Year 5 unit on strong passwords. Knowing how to operate a chatbot isn’t the same as knowing what it is. David Buckingham has argued for a long time that the subject of media education is media, not technology, and his key concepts (production, language, representation and audience) fit an AI answer uncomfortably well. Who produced this, and whose writing was it built from? Why does it sound so sure of itself? What has it left out? And who’s the audience? Just you. Which is exactly the problem.

With ten- and eleven-year-olds the place to start isn’t the technology anyway. Ask a chatbot the same question twice, phrased the way two different children in the class would phrase it, and put both answers side by side on the board. They won’t match word for word, and sometimes they won’t match at all. I’d bet someone says straight away that one of them must be wrong. Then ask the question that matters: which one is true, and how would we find out?

The finding out is the shared map, and it doesn’t belong to the chatbot. The old internet sometimes made us draw one by accident. The new one won’t, so schools will have to do it on purpose, out loud, in a room full of other people.
