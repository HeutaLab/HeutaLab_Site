The Citations Are the Tell.

25 August 2026

An expert witness let ChatGPT write his report. The prose passed. The citations didn’t.

This first went out as Edtech Tuesday #35, a weekly newsletter for teaching staff, on 25 August 2026. Lightly edited for the blog.

The short version. An expert witness in a $61 million lawsuit had ChatGPT write most of his report. The prose was fine; the citations gave him away. A Lancet audit found fake references in published medical papers have risen twelve-fold since 2023. A Northwestern study found one in six sources cited by AI search tools was itself written by AI. The common thread: the writing passes, the sources don’t. Checking sources is becoming the skill that matters most, and it’s one we can teach. There’s a one-lesson activity at the end.

If you have spoken with students about AI recently, the transition of generated output has felt strange. The tools your students use to write are the same ones you use to plan and mark. And under the daily question of “did they write this?” sits a bigger one: what is school for, when any answer could be a prompt away?

I don’t have an answer for that. I do, though, have two stories that I think every teacher should hear. They’re not really about cheating. They’re about what happens when we trust a tool to know things it doesn’t.

The expert who got caught by his footnotes

In 2020 an explosion at a Houston plant killed three people and wrecked around 200 homes. The company that maintained the plant’s gas detection system hired an engineer with twenty years’ experience to write an expert report for the lawsuit. Thirty pages. Ninety thousand dollars.

Last week 404 Media broke the story of how it was written. Nobody caught him because the prose sounded off. A lawyer on the other side found a stray file called “Citation Overlay,” recognised it as ChatGPT output, and asked for every prompt. Mid-questioning, he discovered the engineer’s chat history was still publicly viewable online.

“Do you realize, sir, that your chat right now is still publicly available?”

The engineer said he had no idea how it worked. The prompts asked the AI to show the company was “0% at fault.” Under oath, both sides agreed the report was 85 to 90 percent machine-written.

A seasoned professional with every reason to be careful. The writing looked fine. The sources sank him.

The papers that made it through

In May a Columbia team led by Maxim Topaz published an audit in The Lancet. They checked 97 million references across 2.5 million biomedical papers, asking of each: does the paper it points to exist?

Just over 4,000 didn’t, spread across 2,810 peer-reviewed papers. In 2023 about one paper in 2,800 had a made-up citation. In the first seven weeks of this year, it’s one in 277. The curve turns up in mid-2024, right when AI-drafted papers would have cleared the pipeline. One paper had 18 fake references out of 30. Almost none had been corrected.

Caveats: it’s an early-stage research letter, the checking was automated, and it doesn’t separate references that matter from incidental ones. Take the exact number lightly. The direction is not in doubt.

And a fake reference doesn’t stay put. The next author cites it, trusting the first, and by the third citation nobody remembers it was never real. Students get caught the same way the engineer did: not by tone, but by someone checking the bibliography.

Why the machine keeps doing this

Most big news sites now block AI from reading their pages. So when a student asks an assistant to “research” something, it isn’t reading the internet. It’s reading whatever still lets it in, which is not where the careful reporting lives. It hits a wall, then finds a site called something like Biology Today that says what it wants, and cites that. Clean layout, human byline, entirely machine-written.

Two Northwestern researchers, Mowafak Allaham and Nicholas Diakopoulos, tested this in May. They ran 712 real questions on health, politics and the environment through ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini and Perplexity, then checked the sources cited. About one in six was machine-written. (Caveats: US topics only, and they could read about 73 percent of the links.) Exactly the topics a Year 11 class gets asked to research.

There’s also post hoc citation, where the tool writes the answer and then goes looking for sources to attach. Engineers treat it as a fair design choice. But when the sources are junk, you get a conclusion hunting for evidence. That’s the thing we spend years training out of students.

Where this leaves us

For a long time “finding information” was a skill we taught and then quietly retired, because search engines made it feel solved. I think it’s coming back as the hard part. The value is shifting from “can you find a fact” to “can you vouch for it.” That’s a human skill, and it might be the most important one we teach.

I’d gently push back on bans and detectors. The engineer wasn’t caught by a detector. He was caught by someone who clicked a link and read what was there. That’s teachable.

Something to try this week

Run it as a case

Give students a short AI-generated answer on a topic you know well, citations included, and tell them nothing is confirmed. Their job is to check each source the way a reporter would before printing.

  1. Who’s behind the site? Is there a named person, an About page, an address?
  2. Does the author exist anywhere else with the same name and job?
  3. Do the quotes trace back to an original, or only to sites like this one?
  4. Does the article appear word for word on other domains?

Then sort the evidence into three piles — verified, suspicious, fake. Each pile needs a sentence explaining why.

One lesson, part detective work, part journalism. It’s the habit that would have saved the engineer in Houston. Someone on the other side clicked. He didn’t.
