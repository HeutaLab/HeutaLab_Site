This Time Isn’t Different.

17 September 2026

A short history of fearing the new — from Plato on writing to bicycle face and the Walkman, and why a call for regulation from the firms already at the front deserves a second look.

Why does every new technology seem to arrive with a prophecy of doom attached? AI is only the latest case. The fear is old, the pattern is familiar, and history offers a useful guide to how it usually ends.

An old habit

Expecting the end of the world is one of humanity’s more durable habits. The early Christians expected it within their own lifetimes. Apocalyptic fears gathered around the year 1000, though historians still disagree about how widespread they were. Millenarian movements surged in the 12th and 13th centuries, when followers of Joachim of Fiore fixed on 1260 as a turning point. They surged again in the 17th century, when England’s Fifth Monarchy Men awaited Christ’s imminent rule. Within living memory, the approach of the year 2000 brought fears of a millennium bug. Talk of crashing planes and failing cash machines spread so widely that the UK government issued a leaflet, Facts not Fiction, dismissing both as myths. One survey found that only 60 of 300 people would accept a free flight on 31 December 1999.

The anxiety runs strongest when new technology is involved. Around 370 BC, in Plato’s Phaedrus, Socrates tells of an Egyptian king who feared that writing would implant forgetfulness in learners’ minds and give them only the appearance of wisdom. When printing arrived, governments quickly grasped its political power. In England, a Star Chamber decree of 1637 and the Licensing Act of 1662 brought the press under state licence. That system lapsed in 1695, and state control of print eventually proved unworkable in open societies. Authoritarian regimes such as North Korea and China still control print, and increasingly its digital successors, to keep their populations in line.

A catalogue of false alarms

The list of predicted harms is long, and some of it is entertaining. The popular science writer Dionysius Lardner claimed that a train whose brakes failed in Brunel’s Box Tunnel would accelerate past 120 miles an hour and break up, killing its passengers. Brunel pointed out that Lardner had ignored friction and air resistance. A much-repeated story has Bavarian physicians warning that rail travel would cause “delirium furiosum”. The early motor car raised similar doubts about whether the mind could cope with speed.

Bicycles were blamed for “bicycle face”. An 1894 newspaper report declared that cycling, if persisted in, led to “weakness of mind, general lunacy, and homicidal mania” (more here). Anyone who has watched London’s cyclists at rush hour may suspect the Victorians had a point.

When the Sony Walkman reached America, one Manhattan executive told TIME it was “a great way of snubbing the world”, and meant it as praise. A Detroit psychologist in the same article saw the problem: “These machines are very selfish,” the product of a “me society”, signalling a wish to be left alone. Even Sony’s chairman, Akio Morita, worried about its isolating effect, and at his insistence early models carried a “hot line” button so that two listeners could talk (New Yorker). In 1981 Susan Blond, a vice-president at CBS Records, told the Washington Post: “With the advent of the Sony Walkman came the end of meeting people.” The following year, the same paper’s Walter Shapiro described Walkman wearers as “urban hermits”, each “marching to his own silent drummer”, and saw in the device the shape of a more fragmented, antisocial future. By that summer, a New Jersey township was talking about banning them. The early internet, in its turn, was cast as a source of moral corruption.

Technology has also served as a scapegoat for economic pain. In the Swing Riots of 1830, farm labourers in southern and eastern England smashed threshing machines and sent threatening letters to the farmers who used them. The deeper causes were low wages and the hard times that followed the Napoleonic Wars. The machines were simply the most visible target.

Getting it wrong in the other direction

People misjudge new technology in both directions: they fear it, and they underestimate it. In 1879 William Preece, later engineer-in-chief of the Post Office, told a parliamentary select committee that Britain had little need for the telephone. “Here we have a superabundance of messengers, errand boys, and things of that kind,” he explained (source, extract). In 1896 Lord Kelvin wrote that he had “not the smallest molecule of faith in aerial navigation other than ballooning”. He repeated the point in 1902: “No balloon and no aeroplane will ever be practically successful.” The following year, the Wright brothers flew at Kitty Hawk.

Some of the best-known examples are shakier. The claim that people would “soon get tired of staring at a plywood box every night” is routinely pinned on Darryl Zanuck of 20th Century Fox, but it appears to be a misattribution that stitches together two anonymous remarks. Ken Olsen, founder of Digital Equipment Corporation, is reported to have said in 1977 that he saw no reason for anyone to have a computer at home. His exact words are unrecorded, and he was probably thinking of home terminals linked to larger machines (context). Even allowing for such embellishment, the pattern holds. We routinely underestimate the demand for new technology, people’s ability to adopt it, and how safe it turns out to be.

Why AI feels different, and why it may not be

Anxiety about AI is therefore unsurprising. Fear of the unknown meets the old appetite for apocalypse, and each feeds the other.

A different signal deserves more attention. In May 2023, OpenAI’s chief executive, Sam Altman, told a US Senate committee that AI could be as big as “the printing press”, and called for a new agency to license AI companies. He suggested “a combination of licensing and testing requirements” for models “above a threshold of capabilities” (written testimony). It is the same instinct that produced England’s Licensing Act of 1662. That should set off alarm bells, though not about AI itself. The economist George Stigler argued in 1971 that regulation is, as a rule, “acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit.” A licensing regime shows why. Firms already at the frontier can absorb the cost of compliance, while newcomers face a barrier before they have started. When market leaders ask to be licensed, their motives deserve scrutiny.

The strongest objection is that some of the most serious warnings come from people with no obvious commercial stake. Geoffrey Hinton left Google in 2023 so that he could speak freely about the risks, and Yoshua Bengio now leads the International AI Safety Report. Their concern is that AI is a general-purpose technology capable of acting with growing autonomy, which would set it apart from railways or bicycles. That argument deserves to be taken seriously. It does not, however, make the incumbents’ enthusiasm for regulation any less self-interested. Nor does it, on its own, justify a new regulatory regime when existing law already covers much of the potential harm.

The case for optimism

Time and again, harnessing technology has raised productivity, increased prosperity and made lives easier. AI is already changing science: DeepMind’s AlphaFold predicts protein structures with an accuracy that is feeding directly into drug research. Bad actors will misuse AI, as they misuse every powerful tool, but there are good grounds to expect the benefits to outweigh the harm.

Gunpowder is a useful comparison. It transformed warfare, but the same explosive energy made mining and quarrying far more productive and supplied materials the modern world depends on. Few would wish it uninvented. The sensible response to AI is not a raft of new laws but firm enforcement of existing ones, so that people who use it to cause harm can be stopped.

As for the machines taking over: I say please and thank you to Grok and ChatGPT, but out of habit rather than fear of being switched off. It helps that ChatGPT’s dictation copes well with my sometimes thick Black Country accent.

The Terminator’s vision of Skynet turning the machines against humanity is the classic fear of the new in modern dress. My father remembered teachers who worried that splitting one atom would set off a chain reaction splitting every atom on Earth. That fear, like so many before it, came to nothing. Faced with each new technological leap, history is the better bet. When someone says “this time it’s different”, they are usually barking up the wrong tree.
