Ollie Is Not a Search Box.

9 September 2026

What we are building for the primary school library: an owl that teaches children how to ask, runs on the iPad itself, and forgets everything at the bell.

This is a development update, not a launch. Ollie is being built with one primary school and is not yet available anywhere. Its standing page is on the in-development hub, which is kept current between posts. The screenshots are captures of the real screens from the design rig, with sample answers written in the product’s voice; on the iPad the same screens are answered by a model running on the device.

The short version. Ollie is a learning assistant for the primary school library, for children aged five to eleven. It is not a search box. It is a way of teaching children how to ask — prompting as a taught skill, in the way library skills used to be taught — and a thinking partner that hands the turn back to the child rather than closing the subject. The language model runs on the iPad, so nothing a child says or types is sent to a cloud service. There is no login, no account and nothing stored per child. Four screens, one for each age band, and the same owl grows up across them.

I have spent the summer on this, and it is far enough along to show. What follows is where it stands today, one question all the way through — why do some volcanoes explode and others don’t — so you can see how the same idea is treated at five, at seven and at ten.

Why not a search box

The easy thing to build is a friendly answer machine: a child asks, it answers, everyone moves on. Children already have those at home. What they do not have is anyone teaching them that the quality of what comes back depends on the quality of what went in, that a confident voice is not the same as a right one, and that a book on the shelf three metres away might settle it.

So every design decision in Ollie runs through one rule: when better answers and better asking pull against each other, the asking wins. The answering model is deliberately no cleverer than the ask. It is told not to guess what a vague prompt really meant, so the thinking stays with the child. And at every age the answer ends by passing the turn back — something to go and look at, try, or ask about.

Ages 5–7: Owl Buddy

For the youngest children the tool advances play and talk rather than delivering facts. There is no keyboard for the child and nothing they must read. They press one big yellow button, say what they are wondering, and Ollie talks back — three short sentences by rule, pitched at the reading age the school sets, ending with an “I wonder…” the child could really go and do. The words appear in the speech bubble at speaking pace as the voice reads them, so a child who cannot read yet still sees that Ollie is talking.

Three things about this screen took the most thought. The two to five seconds the on-device model needs are not hidden behind a spinner: Ollie closes his eyes and has a think, because turn-taking is itself a skill this age is learning. The transcript of what the child said is never tidied up or corrected — speech recognition of five-year-olds is far worse than of adults, so Ollie responds to the gist and asks again rather than requiring an accurate transcript. And every turn follows the plan-do-review rhythm of early-years practice: I wonder, let’s find out, I can tell someone. That last step sends the child off to tell a person what they found, which is the review.

For EYFS itself there is no answer step at all. A text-free screen runs a loop of its own where Ollie’s one job is to help the child think and talk about their own ideas. A big why-question is met first with “What do you think?”, a “what should I do?” with “Hmm, what could you try?”, and a muddled word is said back properly but never called wrong.

Ages 7–9: Ollie’s Library

Year 2 is the crossover. Below it the tool advances play and talk; above it the tool supports finding things out. Ollie’s Library is the same owl in daylight: the child now reads and types, the library’s own shelves come into the picture, and the habit this band takes away is the third beat — check it before you use it.

The mechanism underneath is retrieval before generation. The question is scored against the school’s own catalogue export and the top three books are written into the model’s instructions, so the answer can point at a real shelf with a real call number. Then the badge checks whether it really did. Green — “A book on our shelves backs this up” — only if the answer names one of the retrieved titles. Otherwise an amber flag: “Ollie’s own words, check before you trust it.” A child can press How sure are you? and Ollie has to say how sure, where it came from, and one thing to check in a real book. At seven, thinking with Ollie means not taking his word for it.

Ages 9–11: Nexus, and Prompt Lab

By upper Key Stage 2 the owl has gone. Nexus Library Desk treats the child as a researcher: a query console, the response with its sources, the matching shelf, and a running count of how the session is going — questions asked, books surfaced, follow-ups taken, answers with a source. Switch on Ask me first and the model is told not to answer but to ask one good question back. Press Inspect prompt and the exact instructions the model was given open up, books and all. At this age the mechanism is part of what is being taught, so nothing is hidden from a child who wants to see how it works.

Prompt Lab is the core of the whole product. A child brings one real question of their own — for a poster, a talk, a project — and works it through six rounds, each practising one prompting skill: Be specific · Say who it’s for · Ask for a shape · Set a limit · Follow up · Check it. Every round they write a prompt, see what the model gives back, and get coached. The coach marks the prompt, never the answer.

Two separate model calls run each round. One answers the child’s prompt as the school librarian would. The second is the coach: it is told the real question, the audience, the reading age and the skill being practised, and it must reply in a strict form — which ingredients the prompt has, whether it passes, one tip, one rewrite under thirty-five words. Passing is a rule, not an opinion. Round one requires a task and a topic; a child cannot skip a skill by writing something charming. The last round, Catch it being wrong, teaches that the model will say wrong things in a confident voice, so make it show its working.

What a child leaves with is a research sheet: their question, their best prompt, the answers they chose to keep, and two boxes only they can fill — “in my own words” and “still don’t know”. It can be copied or printed. It never goes anywhere by itself.

True on every screen

The four promises the whole thing rests on

  • On the iPad, not the cloud. A small open language model runs on the device itself. No analytics, no telemetry, no crash reporter that could capture a transcript. The one declared exception is a printed take-home sheet where a school has switched printing on — and the privacy badge turns amber at that moment, so the exception is stated, not smuggled.
  • Zero teacher setup. No login, no settings screen, no model picker. Configuration arrives centrally through the school’s device management. The one cog on screen is a PIN-gated panel for a practitioner to change the voice or language for the next group.
  • Forgets at the bell. Library iPads are shared roughly one between two. Nothing is stored per child; a session clears when the app closes, and the screen says so to the child in plain words rather than in a policy.
  • Two update rhythms. Screens, prompts, content and the book index can change any day. The model and the voice ship termly with the school’s app list, so nothing changes underneath a class mid-term.

Where it is, and what is next

The four screens are designed and running in a browser rig at iPad width, which is where the screenshots above come from. Inside the iPad app the same screens call the on-device model through a bridge; the HTML does not change. The pieces still being built are the ones that make it honest in a real library rather than a demonstration:

  • The reviewed canon. The common why-questions have teacher-reviewed answers that are matched before the model is asked at all; the model is the honest fallback for the long tail. A hundred-concept early-years corpus is being written to widen that canon, and unanswered questions are logged on the device as a coverage signal for the teacher.
  • The school’s own shelves. The books in the rig are sample catalogue entries. In a school they come from the library’s catalogue export, and getting that import boring and reliable is most of the remaining work on the library screens.
  • Voice. Speech in is Apple’s on-device recogniser; speech out is a neural voice rendered on the device, split at sentence boundaries with real pauses and a longer breath before any sentence beginning “I wonder”. Playback always stops before the microphone opens, so the iPad never transcribes Ollie instead of the child. Tuning that for a noisy library is ongoing.

What I’d love from you

If you run a primary library, or teach in one of these year groups, two things would help enormously. First, the questions your children actually ask — the real ones, misspelt and half-formed — because those are what the reviewed canon should be built from. Second, the moment you would want Ollie to refuse, or to hand a child back to you. The design gets better every time someone tells me where the line should be.

I will post again when there is something to put in your hands.
