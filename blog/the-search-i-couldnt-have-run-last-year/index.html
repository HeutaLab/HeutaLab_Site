The Search I Couldn’t Have Run Last Year.

1 September 2026

A scout persona found fifteen iPad lessons search never could — and got two of them wrong, fluently.

This first went out as Edtech Tuesday #36, a weekly newsletter for teaching staff, on 1 September 2026. Lightly edited for the blog.

The short version. Search is dead. I gave GrokBot (Grok’s new agentic AI platform) a scout persona and one narrow question: what are primary teachers actually doing with iPads and images right now? It found around fifteen documented lessons across five countries, including a freelance illustrator’s write-up of a Year 6 workshop in Cheshire that nothing else would have surfaced in a regular search, AI or otherwise. The results were nothing short of superb. The way it can chain together a resource search, much like Grok can through X.com, but this collates and repeats your requests. For teachers, this is excellent; much like any AI, it’s not infallible. Links and resources at the end.

A colleague asked me something last week that I answered badly: if I was to use my iPad again in class, where might I look for inspiration?

Naming a blog is easy. Knowing whether anyone still works that way is not, because primary practice isn’t published anywhere findable. It sits on class blogs and school newsletters — unindexed, badly titled, no ranking. Google won’t show you what a Year 2 class in Auckland did on Tuesday.

The scout

So I gave GrokBot a persona — a scout, reporting back to me over several hours with finds — written for this one question: primary classrooms, iPads, images, show me the lesson and not the product, because I don’t want apps, I want the learning experiences.

It is superb. Fifteen lessons came back, 2019 to June: Apple Education stories, New Zealand class blogs, teacher blogs from Australia and Cornwall, a Shanghai portfolio, US local news.

And one I keep coming back to: a freelance illustrator’s account of digital portrait workshops with Year 6 at Rode Heath Primary in Cheshire, and again at Birkby Junior. One artist, one small site, no ranking, no reason anyone would find it. That page is the argument for these tools.

What they showed

Most are the same lesson: a drawing app, a picture, one session.

Cathy Hunt, who wrote iPad Art Room until 2020, worked differently. Her move was a loop. Children photograph something. It gets printed on A3 cartridge — heavier than photocopy paper, she specifies, so they have room. Then they go over the print by hand.

Only two of the fifteen ever print anything, and the better one is from 2019: a Year 5–6 class in Australia photographing their own houses, tracing the outlines, printing on A3, painting them in Howard Arkley’s colours. Hunt’s loop is almost always the creative part, with any app used as a tool.

And it isn’t about paint, or even really about printing. The move is that the digital thing hands off to something else. A leaf photographed and then labelled on paper. A Canva design marked up by hand. A video that becomes a live retelling. A voiceover drafted on screen and then performed to the class. A poem typed, printed, cut into strips and reordered on a desk. Any subject, any topic, any medium — what matters is that it leaves the glass.

Everywhere else I see online, the work starts on a screen and finishes there. What survived is the half that lives inside the app — a Year 2–3 class in Auckland photograph themselves, lasso themselves out, draw a poppy on for ANZAC Day. That’s Hunt’s tracing lesson, unaltered. It just stops one step early.

Where the scout was wrong

Last week I wrote to secondary about this issue… It told me the Illinois piece was about Procreate self-portraits in the style of Van Gogh. The article names no app, no portraits, no Van Gogh. It listed a Sketchbook selfie lesson as a class; it’s an after-school club with no ages given. The link in our school can easily be done on Tayasui Sketches School.

Both read perfectly well. Neither was visible until I clicked. Issue 35’s phantom link problem, arriving at my desk.

Where this leaves us

Hunt’s writing survives because she recorded processes, not products. Half the apps she names are dead or subscription-only — ColorLight Pro gone, Adobe pulled Illustrator Draw in 2021 — however every lesson still runs.

GrokBot is the same shape. The bot is the product. The persona was the process, and that’s the part that did the work, and the part I’ll reuse next week on something else entirely. Extraordinary at finding, unreliable at describing. Let it reach what search can’t, then open every link yourself. Try the free trial, or just Grok itself for an in-app search.

Nobody has reinvented the printer. It costs nothing to put back.

Something to try this week

Close the loop

Any subject, any year group, whatever drawing app you have.

  1. Children photograph the thing you want them to look at properly. A leaf. A pulley. The front of the school. Their own face.
  2. Open the photo, add a layer above it in Markup, trace only the outlines. Label the items. Hide the photo layer. What’s left is theirs, not the camera’s.
  3. Print it on A3.
  4. Put the iPad away and work on the paper. In art that’s paint and collage. In science it’s labels; in maths it’s shape, space and measure, and what you couldn’t see from outside in a human body drawing. In geography it’s process arrows. In DT it’s what you’d change and why. Try these ideas with Grok search and see what other educators have made.

Step 2 gets the children who don’t believe they can draw past the blank page — the point is the observing. Step 3 is the one everyone has dropped, and it’s what moves the thinking off the glass and onto a desk.
