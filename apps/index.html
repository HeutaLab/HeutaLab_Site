Apps

Everything built here, in one place.

None of these started as a product. Each one began as a job that was taking too long — recording what a class could actually do, running the screen at the front of the room, filling in the planning format, getting thirty children coding without thirty installs — and was built in the gaps of a real timetable by the person doing the job. That is why they are shaped the way they are, and why none of them asks you to make an account before it will show you anything.

For the teacher

The jobs that eat the week.

Assessment, the screen at the front of the room, and the planning document. Each one opens on something already working, so you can see what it does before committing a class to it.

C Creance

Assessment recorded while you teach, not after.

Paste in a class list, add your own success criteria, and mark against them as you move around the room. Every child's picture builds up criterion by criterion, so the gaps show themselves before the end of the unit rather than after it.

  • Mark as Not started, Developing, Secure or Mastered — in Grid, Focus or Tap mode.
  • High achiever and encouragement flags appear on their own, next to the children they apply to.
  • Class breakdown and analytics show how the whole group is doing at a glance.
  • Export the lot as a spreadsheet when reports are due.
Open the example → Start an empty copy

Opens on two invented classes and a term of marks, so there is something to look at. Your school's own copy at creance.heutalab.com starts empty and keeps its data separate.

S Sage on the Stage

The screen at the front of the room.

Around fifty widgets — timers, name picker, group maker, traffic light, draw pad, teaching clock, noise meter — that you drop onto a screen and arrange however the lesson needs. Build a deck ahead of time, or add a tool the moment you need it.

  • Screens you can prepare as a deck, so a lesson's tools are one tap apart.
  • Paste a class register once and every name tool uses it.
  • Nothing is recorded or sent anywhere — the noise meter and webcam included.
  • Every widget is documented, with a shareable link straight to any one of them.

Opens on a demonstration class, so the name tools have something to pick from. The reference at sagestage.co.uk documents every widget and links straight to any one of them.

P PlanBench

Your school’s planning format, as an app.

The nine headings you already fill in each term, with the resources clipped to the week they are used, the success criteria written once in your words and once in the child’s, and the finished plan out the other end as the Word or PDF the school asked for. Built by a teacher for the format their own school uses.

  • One job ticket per week, stamped guided, fading or flying — change the unit length and the spiral re-cuts itself.
  • Criteria become a class checklist, a rubric sheet, or a marking grid sent straight to Creance.
  • Draft any section with AI that has read your own policies and scheme of work first — on your own key, stored on the device.
  • Exports to Word and PDF, imports from Word, and saves to a real folder in OneDrive or Drive.

F Flect

The room on one screen.

The Mac at the front becomes the place the iPads go. A child opens Control Centre, taps Screen Mirroring, and their work is on the board — or beside nineteen other children’s, each tile labelled with whose it is.

  • Up to twenty-four iPads at once; click one to fill the screen, and its sound comes with it.
  • Ask before an iPad appears, and let a whole class on with one click.
  • Silence one iPad, or all of them, without touching anyone’s iPad.
  • Keep the work: a snapshot as a picture, or a recording with its sound.
Download Flect → How it works

A Mac app, free and open source, for macOS 14 or later. Nothing to install on the iPads and no accounts anywhere. Pictures and recordings stay in the teacher’s own folders; nothing about a lesson leaves the Mac.

L LoopLab

Children write real code, and hear whether it works.

The one they open themselves. Six levels take a class from their first note to two loops running at once — then the club opens, and the tracks in there came back from the studio with bugs to be found by ear and fixed live. Ages 9–13.

  • Every keyword is the real Sonic Pi keyword, and the finished code pastes into the free desktop app and runs.
  • Blocks to begin with, typing by the end — each stage marks itself and pays out up to three stars.
  • Debugging you can hear: the test is the child’s own ear, not a green tick.
  • Up to 40 named players per device, and a summary of the work to paste into Google Classroom.

It makes a noise, so it asks for headphones before it will start — and carries a mute mode for demonstrating on the board. Nothing a child types leaves the device.

P Perkush

A drum kit made out of whatever is in the room.

The youngest app here, and the only one where the first job is to go and find a sound. A clap, a stomp, a pencil tin, their own voice going boing — the camera catches it, the pad keeps it, and tapping the pad plays it back.

  • Record a pad with the camera, so the sound and the face that made it both land on it.
  • Fifteen cartoon instruments for the days there is no quiet room and no time.
  • Make a beat records a rhythm and loops it, so they can play on top of themselves.
  • Boards are named and swapped — one for the kitchen, one for animals, one for the playground.
Open Perkush → What it does with the camera

Camera and microphone come on only while a pad is recording, and the browser asks first. Nothing is uploaded — though a saved board file holds the recordings themselves, faces and voices included.
