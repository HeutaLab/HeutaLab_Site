Apps · Perkush

Perkush.

Make a drum kit out of anything. Record a clap, a stomp or a silly noise with the camera, drop it on a pad, and play your own band.

A drum machine usually arrives with the drums already in it. Perkush arrives empty, and the first thing a child does is go and find a sound. A clap. A stomp on the floor. A pencil tin. Their own voice going boing. The camera catches it, the pad keeps it — sound and picture both — and from then on tapping that pad plays back the noise they made and the face they made while making it.

That is the whole idea, and it is the reason this is an app rather than a box of instruments. The kit is theirs because they went and got it.

Open Perkush What it does with the camera

A pad is whatever you point the camera at

Tap an empty pad and the choice is the first thing on the screen: record your own, or take one of the ready-made instruments. Recording opens the camera, the child makes their noise, and it lands on the pad as a short clip. Tap the pad and it plays. Tap it fast and you get a drum roll.

  • Silly looks. While the camera is on there is a sparkle button — wobbly, rainbow, blocky, big-headed. Whatever is chosen is what the pad keeps.
  • Short is better. Claps, knocks, pops and boings make good drums; a sentence does not. The app says so on its own front page.
  • Wrong sound? Edit, then Change or Clear, and the pad goes back to being empty.

Fifteen instruments, for the days you have five minutes

Recording needs a reasonably quiet room and a child who has decided what their sound is. Neither is guaranteed on a wet Tuesday, so the app ships with fifteen instruments that work straight away — big drum, snare, hi-hat, cymbal, clap, cowbell, tambourine, maracas, triangle, woodblock, bongos, xylophone, gong, boing and pop. Each one is a cartoon with a face and a noise written underneath it, so a child who cannot yet read the word tambourine can still find the jingle. They mix freely with recorded pads: three real instruments and one recording of a slammed drawer is a perfectly good band.

Make a beat, then play on top of it

The red button along the top is the part that turns a soundboard into an instrument. Press it and it counts down — four, three, two, one — then records whatever is tapped out. Press it again and that rhythm loops round and round, and the child plays over the top of themselves. In too much of a hurry to count? Tapping a pad starts it.

Drum along is the other half of the same idea: a steady boom-tak at slow, medium or fast, so there is a pulse to play against before there is a loop. Slow is the one to start on. A beat recorded while drum along is running begins on a beat rather than wherever the child's finger landed, which is the difference between a rhythm and a scramble.

Boards, and keeping them

A board is a kit. One for the kitchen, one for animals, one for the playground — each named, and swapped between from the menu at the top. A board can be saved out as a single file, which is how a kit moves to another tablet, or survives one being wiped.

Worth knowing before a child spends a lesson on it: the pads live in the browser's own storage, and Safari clears that if the site is not opened for a week. Adding the page to the Home Screen (Share, then Add to Home Screen) and saving any board worth keeping as a file are the two things that stop good work disappearing over half term.

For grown-ups

Where the recordings go

Nowhere. Perkush has no account, no server and no upload. The camera and microphone are switched on only while a pad is actually recording — the browser asks first, and the answer can be no without breaking the rest of the app — and what they capture is written into storage on that device.

The one thing to be deliberate about is saving a board. That file contains the recordings themselves, faces and voices included. It goes where your downloads go, on that device, and it reaches another person only if someone sends it. Worth a thought before a board of a class of five-year-olds is emailed anywhere.

It wants Safari on an iPad or iPhone, or Chrome. There is nothing to install and nothing to log in to; the link is the app.

Try it

Open it on a tablet, tap a pad, and make a noise at it. The first recording takes about fifteen seconds, and everything else in the app follows from it.
