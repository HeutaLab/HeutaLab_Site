Paws & Order

Teaching with Paws & Order

Paws & Order is a guided comic-making workshop for younger learners. It teaches them to look closely, describe precisely, keep a character, a place and their colours the same from picture to picture, judge what an AI makes, and turn their improved pictures into a story.

The learning is the human thinking around the AI, not the AI: close observation, precise language, consistency, comparing and trying again, and making a choice on purpose instead of taking the first result. Paws & Order writes text prompts. It never makes the pictures.

  • Close observation
  • Precise description
  • Visual storytelling
  • AI literacy

Ages 9 to 12. One lesson of about 55 minutes. In one lesson expect one picture each; Sketcher and Storyteller sets, and a full comic page, need a second lesson.

Suggested lesson: about 55 minutes

The lesson follows the five steps learners see on every page, in the same order. Most of the work happens on Paws & Order. In step 3 learners take a prompt to your image tool and make the picture there. In step 4 they ask the tool to describe that picture.

Before they start

Put the code on the board first: learners type it on the home page before they start, so the right levels are open from the beginning.

Learning intentions and success criteria

Learning intentions

Learners are learning to:

  • analyse visual language;
  • describe visible details precisely;
  • maintain character, setting and colour consistency;
  • evaluate the gap between an intention and an AI-generated result;
  • revise one variable deliberately;
  • sequence images and words into a story.

Success criteria

You can see the learning when learners:

  • describe before they prompt;
  • separate observation from invented story details;
  • repeat stable character and place descriptions;
  • explain which work happens on Paws & Order and which happens in the image tool;
  • identify one specific mismatch;
  • make one purposeful change and explain why;
  • use panels, captions and speech to communicate a sequence.

Differentiation

  • Doodler

    Level 1

    One character portrait. Focus on observable features.

  • Sketcher

    Level 2

    The same character in a place. Focus on consistency.

  • Storyteller

    Level 3

    Several connected images. Focus on shot planning, continuity and sequence.

You choose the highest level the class can use when you set up the prompt helper. Learners see the levels above it as locked. With a workshop code you can change the level during the lesson.

Extra activity: Spot the AI slips

Two of the site’s pictures, the Robot Chef and the Doughnut Factory, were made by an AI image tool and have mistakes left in. Spot the AI slips asks learners to find one, say why it is a problem, and choose one thing to change. It takes about ten minutes and works as a warm-up for step 1, or just before step 4, where learners do the same with their own picture.

Learners answer in three parts: I notice…, It may be a problem because…, I would change… What they write stays on their device.

How to run it

Do not start with the answers. Ask learners to point to what they can see, and to tell a cartoon exaggeration that was meant from a mistake that was not.

Robot Chef: questions to ask

  • Can each claw really grip its tool?
  • Are the arm joints built the same way on both sides?
  • Where do the apron ties join, and would they stay tied when the robot moves?
  • Do the shoulder plates, screws and chest dial sit on one body you can believe in?
  • Could another artist draw the same robot from the side, using only this picture?

Doughnut Factory: questions to ask

  • Trace the conveyor belt from start to end. Does every join make sense?
  • Which machine makes, cooks, decorates or packs the doughnuts?
  • Do the pipes join a machine that has a job to do?
  • Could the trolley reach the belt, and where would a worker stand?
  • Do the sizes and the angle of view stay the same across the room?

The Robot Chef is also a picture in Look Closely. There learners only say what they can see, so its slips do not get in the way; this activity is where they are named.

Set up the prompt helper

No key and no code?

The lesson still works. Learners get a starter prompt made from their own brief, and check their picture by eye. The prompt helper adds coaching questions and a tuned prompt; it is not required.

The prompt helper is the AI on Paws & Order that turns a learner’s brief into a prompt, and compares what they asked for with what they got. There are three ways to switch it on. Use one.

AA. Make a workshop code (recommended)

You give your own AI key once. The site gives you a code of three words and a number. Learners type the code, and never see or handle the key.

  • You need an API key from Anthropic, OpenAI or Google. That is a developer account with a payment card, not a ChatGPT or Gemini subscription.
  • Allow 15 minutes the first time, so do it the day before.
  • Make a key just for this, set a spend limit on it, and delete it at the AI service afterwards.
  • A lesson for one class usually costs about one US dollar or less on the recommended model; check your service’s prices.
  • Brand-new and free keys can be too slow for 30 learners at once: if learners get starter prompts instead, that is why.
  • Make the code on your own device, not one learners use.

Type it exactly as the AI service writes it, for example in its list of models. Use this if the models in the list have been retired.

It goes to heutalab.com once, is tested with one short message to the AI service, and is stored there encrypted. This page never keeps it: the box is emptied as soon as the server answers.

You can change it later without making a new code.

Learners are then told which tool, and do not choose.

A learner uses about 3 to 6 in a lesson.

Good to know

  • Whatever a learner types (their description, their idea, pasted text in the check) goes from this browser to the AI service you chose. Nothing is stored or seen by us. Check that service’s rules for children’s data.
  • Use a key with a low spending limit. Anyone who can open this browser’s developer tools on this device can read a saved key.
  • The helper is told to keep everything friendly and to invent original characters, and a few adult words are blocked. It is a backstop: an adult should still be in the room.
  • A workshop code typed on this device is used in place of this key until the code ends or is forgotten.

Privacy and data

No learner names, school names, email addresses or profiles are asked for anywhere. There are no learner accounts.

On this device only

Progress, descriptions, ideas, prompts, the check, the chosen improvement, the reflection, comics and the pictures added to the Comic Maker are kept on this device only. So are any characters a learner makes. They stay in this browser unless the prompt helper is used as below.

With a workshop code

When a learner types the code, the code and a random id for the browser go to heutalab.com’s server (a Cloudflare Worker) to be checked. The device then remembers the code until it ends, and for no more than 12 hours after its last check.

When a learner presses Get my prompt or Compare them, this goes to the server:

  • their description, their idea and their choices (character or place, level, colours and image tool);
  • the name and look of a character they made, never its picture;
  • the workshop code, and a random id for the browser, which is not a person;
  • for Compare them: the prompt, the pasted descriptions and the helper’s earlier prompts.

From there the learner’s text and choices go to the AI service the teacher chose (Anthropic, OpenAI or Google), on the teacher’s key. The server passes the text on and does not store or log it. The code, the browser id and the helper’s earlier prompts are used by the server and are not passed on. The description of a reference picture is passed on only at Storyteller.

The AI service handles what it receives under the terms of the teacher’s own account, which may include keeping it for a time: check those terms before the lesson. Some free keys (for example the free tier of Google AI Studio) let the service use what is sent to improve its products, so use a paid or school key.

What the server keeps for a code a teacher made

  • the teacher’s API key, encrypted. The site owner holds the unlocking secret, so make a key just for this, set a spend limit, and delete it at the AI service afterwards;
  • the level, image tool, AI service and model;
  • when the code was made, when it ends and whether it was ended;
  • the daily allowance and today’s count of helper requests;
  • a scrambled fingerprint of the code and of the teacher’s manage key. The code itself is not kept.

The encrypted key is erased when the code is ended, or within a day of its expiry (at most 30 days after it was made). Cloudflare’s database backups can hold the encrypted copy for up to 30 days more. What is left of the record is deleted 30 days after the code finished.

To slow misuse the server also counts requests: per internet address and per browser id for about a minute, per browser id (scrambled) for up to six hours, and how many keys were tested and codes made in a day. These are counters, not a log. If a request fails, the server’s log gets the name of the request and a short code for what went wrong. No names, no learner text, no per-request record.

On the teacher’s device

The device a code was made on keeps the code, its settings and a manage key, so that you can end the code or change its level. It never keeps the API key.

Own-key mode (advanced)

Text goes straight from the browser to that AI service; nothing passes through heutalab.com. The key is kept in that browser: until the tab closes, or until you press Forget the key if you chose to remember it.

Pictures

Pictures are made in a separate image tool chosen by the school. Paws & Order never receives them. A picture a learner adds to the Comic Maker, or to a character they made, stays on that device.

To clear a device, see Shared devices.

Shared devices

A learner’s work is saved in the browser of the device they used. Before another class uses the same devices, clear it with Start again.

What Start again clears

  • Always: the learner’s progress, description, idea, prompts and check, and the random id the browser uses with the prompt helper.
  • When its box is ticked: the saved comics and the page on the desk in the Comic Maker; the characters the learner made; the workshop code.
  • Never: a key saved with option C. Use Forget the key there.

This device

Every box starts ticked, and here there is one more: “Also forget the workshop I made on this device”. If you made your code on this device and still want to end it or change its level, untick that box before you press Yes.

A class set

At the end, show this address big: heutalab.com/paws-and-order/?reset=1. Each learner opens it and presses Yes, start again.

Ending the workshop also stops every learner’s code: a device drops the code the next time it checks it, and in any case 12 hours after its last check.

If you made the code on a device learners use, press Forget this workshop on this device as well.

The image tool

Paws & Order writes the words. The picture is made in a separate image tool, which you choose.

  • Choose one your school has approved, and check its age and data rules.
  • Most image tools ask users to be 13 or older, or to use a school account. These learners are 9 to 12.
  • Learners never type names or personal details into it.
  • Paws & Order never receives pictures.
Step 3 as a class

If learners may not use the tool themselves, run step 3 as a class: choose one learner’s prompt, make the picture on your screen, and everyone checks it by eye.

Accessibility

In the Comic Maker, moving and resizing pictures and speech bubbles needs a mouse or touch. So does choosing a caption, speech bubble or sticker again once it has been let go, to restyle or delete it. Plan for a partner or an adult to help a learner who uses only the keyboard with that part.
